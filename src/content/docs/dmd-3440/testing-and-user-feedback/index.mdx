You've designed and built your app. Now the hard part: finding out whether it actually works for other people. **Usability testing** is watching real users try your app — and it's the fastest way to find problems you'll never spot yourself.

## Usability Testing Methods

**Usability testing** means observing a user working through your app while you watch (and don't help). It's not a demo — you're looking for where they struggle.

A simple, effective format:

1. **Choose a task** — "Add a route to your favorites, then check tomorrow's schedule"
2. **Ask the user to complete it** — out loud, thinking aloud as they go
3. **Watch silently** — no hints, no rescuing. Note everything they hesitate on or get wrong
4. **Debrief** — ask what was confusing and what they expected to happen

Even **5 users** catches most major problems. You don't need a lab — a classmate, a quiet room, and a phone will do.

## Gathering User Feedback

Beyond a formal test, gather feedback from many directions:

- **Peer critique** — classmates review your project; you review theirs
- **Think-aloud sessions** — capture users' running commentary as they use your app
- **Surveys / quick questions** — "What was the most confusing part?"
- **Beta testers** — let a few people use the app freely for a day, then interview them

Record feedback as **observations**, not opinions: "the user tapped the logo expecting to go home" beats "the user didn't like it."

## Identifying Friction Points

As you watch, look for **friction points** — moments where the user pauses, frowns, mis-taps, or asks for help. Common ones in mobile apps:

- **Hidden actions** — features users can't find
- **Unexpected behavior** — taps that don't do what users expected
- **Too many steps** — tasks that take longer than they should
- **Unclear labels** — text that means something different to users than to you

Mark each observation with *where* it happened and *why* it mattered. This evidence is what makes your revisions defensible.

## Prioritizing Revisions

You'll collect more feedback than you can fix. **Prioritize** with two questions:

1. **How severe?** — does it block the task, frustrate, or just annoy?
2. **How common?** — how many users hit it, how often?

A quick prioritization grid:

| | Rare | Common |
|---|---|---|
| **Severe** | Fix if cheap | **Fix now** |
| **Annoying** | Track it | Fix this round |

Fix the *severe + common* items first, then the cheap wins. Log the rest for future versions. Perfect is the enemy of shipped.

## Lab: Peer Testing Sessions

This section covers structured peer testing:

1. **You test** — run a usability test on a classmate's project (with their permission)
2. **They test yours** — swap, and gather their feedback
3. **Write it up** — produce a brief **user testing report** listing:
   - What you asked the user to do
   - What you observed (the friction points)
   - What you'll fix, in priority order

**Deliverable:** your brief user testing report.

### Final Project Focus

Use the findings to drive your final polish: fix the prioritized issues, complete the remaining features, and get your app as close to finished as you can before deployment and presentation.
