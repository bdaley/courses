## UX is how it feels to use

**User experience (UX)** is simply how a person feels when they use a product or service. For us, that product is almost always a **mobile app**.

Think of it like a restaurant. The food matters — but so do the wait time, the noise, the menu you can actually read, and whether the server listens. Diners remember the whole evening, not just the plate. App users are the same: they remember the whole experience, not just one screen.

You can add every feature imaginable to an app. Success still rides on three questions:

- Does the app give the user **value**?
- Is it **simple** to use and navigate?
- Is it actually **enjoyable** to use?

A UX designer can claim a good job only when the answer is "yes" to all three.

## UX vs. UI — don't confuse them

**User interface (UI)** is the specific surface a person touches: buttons, type, color, spacing, icons, and layout. **UX** is the whole journey that surface serves — including what happens before and after the tap.

A bus-tracking app with beautiful buttons still has terrible UX if the arrival times are wrong. A plain-looking app with accurate times and one-tap saving can have great UX.

## What does a UX designer do?

A **UX designer** investigates how users feel about a product, then uses that knowledge to guide what the team builds. In practice that means:

1. **Research** — watch and ask real users (interviews, observation)
2. **Analyze** — turn notes into patterns: goals, pain points, needs
3. **Share** — tell designers and developers what matters most
4. **Verify** — check the built app against the findings and iterate

On a small class team, you play all four roles. That's normal — startups work the same way.

## Why UX matters

In the early web, there was little competition — designers could build what *they* thought was cool and hope users showed up. That era is over.

On mobile, the stakes are higher than on desktop:

- **Alternatives are one tap away.** If your app confuses someone, the App Store and Play Store offer five replacements.
- **Attention is fragmented.** People use phones on buses, in lines, mid-conversation. Confusion plus distraction equals abandonment.
- **Ratings are public.** A frustrating release becomes a one-star review that future users read before installing.

Designing for UX doesn't gamble on brand loyalty. It earns success by fitting real user needs.

## Where you'll encounter UX work

UX matters most in four project situations — all of which describe this course:

- **Complex projects** — more features handled badly drives users away faster
- **Small teams / startups** — no dedicated UX team, so everyone owns UX (that's you)
- **Projects with real budgets** — teams spend on UX to protect their investment
- **Long projects** — like your final project, where early UX mistakes compound

## The method: user-centered design

The main methodology is **user-centered design**: design with users' needs and expected behaviors in mind from the start — not as decoration at the end. It is *a* means to good UX, not the only tool, but it is the one this course follows:

Research users → define the problem → imagine solutions → prototype → test → repeat.

You'll practice the full loop in [User-Centered Design](../user-centered-design/).
